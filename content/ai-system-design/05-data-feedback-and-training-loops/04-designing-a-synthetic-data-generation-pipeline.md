## The problem

Real labelled data is scarce, slow and expensive, and sometimes sensitive. A strong model can **generate training or evaluation examples** to bootstrap a smaller model, cover rare cases, or augment limited data. But generated data is only as good as its diversity and correctness: left unchecked it is repetitive, subtly wrong and biased toward the generator's habits. The pipeline's job is to produce data that is **diverse, correct and useful**, and to prove it.

## Step 1: Requirements

- **Purpose-driven:** fine-tuning data for a task, evaluation sets, safety red-team data, or data augmentation, each with different quality bars.
- **Quality:** high correctness, controlled style, coverage of the real input distribution including edge cases.
- **Diversity:** many distinct scenarios, not thousands of near-duplicates.
- **Governance:** no leakage of real personal data; respect model-provider terms on using outputs for training.
- **Scale (example):** 500,000 candidate examples, 50,000 kept.

## Step 2: Pipeline stages

1. **Specification:** define the task, input and output formats, the real distribution you want to match, and quality criteria.
2. **Seeding:** collect a small set of real or expert-written examples and a taxonomy of topics, personas, difficulty levels and edge cases.
3. **Generation:** prompt a strong model to create examples from seeds and taxonomy cells.
4. **Filtering and verification:** remove duplicates, invalid formats and incorrect examples.
5. **Scoring and selection:** rank by quality and diversity, choose the final set.
6. **Evaluation:** train or test with it and compare against real data baselines.
7. **Iteration:** analyse failures, adjust the taxonomy and prompts, regenerate.

## Step 3: Generating diverse data

A single prompt repeated yields clustered, bland outputs. Techniques for diversity:

- **Taxonomy-driven generation:** enumerate cells (topic by persona by difficulty by language) and generate per cell, ensuring coverage.
- **Seed evolution:** take existing examples and ask the model to make them harder, shorter, more ambiguous or in a new domain.
- **Varied sampling:** higher temperature and different generator models and prompts.
- **Negative and edge cases:** deliberately generate invalid inputs, refusals, ambiguous questions and adversarial cases.
- **Persona and style variation** so the language is not uniform.

Measure diversity explicitly: embed examples and check coverage and cluster spread; deduplicate near-copies.

## Step 4: Ensuring correctness

Models make confident errors, so verify. Where possible use **objective checks**: execute generated code against tests, validate JSON schemas, check arithmetic with a calculator, verify retrieval-grounded answers against the source passages. Where answers are subjective, use a **judge model with a rubric** and a different (ideally stronger) model than the generator, plus **consistency checks** (generate multiple answers and keep only agreed ones). Sample outputs for **human review** and use the results to calibrate the automatic filters.

## Step 5: Avoiding the traps

- **Model collapse:** training repeatedly on a model's own outputs narrows its distribution and amplifies errors. Keep real data in the mix, use a stronger teacher than the student, and never evaluate on synthetic data generated by the same family you trained.
- **Style imprinting:** the student copies the teacher's quirks (verbosity, stock phrases). Vary and filter style.
- **Distribution mismatch:** synthetic inputs look cleaner than real user messages. Mix in real examples and add realistic noise (typos, slang, partial context).
- **Test contamination:** ensure generated training data does not duplicate evaluation items.
- **Bias amplification:** audit demographic and topical balance.
- **Terms and licensing:** check what the generator's licence allows you to do with outputs.

## Step 6: Privacy-preserving use

Synthetic data can stand in for sensitive records, but it can leak: a generator conditioned on real records may reproduce them. Generate from schemas and aggregates, not from raw personal data, run memorisation and re-identification checks, and apply redaction and policy review before release.

## Step 7: Measuring usefulness

Ultimately, what matters is downstream performance. Train small ablations: real only, synthetic only, mixed, and compare on a held-out **real** evaluation set. Track which taxonomy cells or generation methods contribute gains, and drop those that do not. Log the provenance of every example (generator, prompt version, filters passed) for debugging and audit.

## A worked example

**Scenario:** a team wants to fine-tune a small model to classify support tickets into 40 categories but has only 800 labelled tickets.

1. They build a taxonomy: 40 categories by 6 customer types by 4 tones, including rare categories with only a handful of real examples.
2. A strong model generates 12 tickets per cell using five real examples as few-shot seeds, at varied temperatures. That yields about 115,000 candidates.
3. Filters remove duplicates (embedding similarity above 0.95), invalid labels, and tickets a second model labels differently; 40 percent survive. A human reviews 300 samples and finds 94 percent correctly labelled.
4. Selection balances categories and favours diversity, producing 20,000 synthetic examples mixed with the 800 real ones.
5. The fine-tuned model reaches 91 percent accuracy on a held-out set of real tickets versus 78 percent for real data alone; the pipeline's provenance records show which cells helped most.

## Enterprise practice (verified October 2026)

**Basics.** Seed with real examples, generate with a strong model, filter, deduplicate, validate, mix into training (steps above).

**What the evidence says.**

- **Model collapse is real when synthetic data replaces real data.** Recursive training on a model's own outputs loses diversity and amplifies errors. Secondary summaries of the 2026 literature converge on mitigation: **accumulate** real data alongside synthetic rather than replacing it, keep a nontrivial real-data fraction in every generation (one line of work cites roughly 20 to 30% or more as a safeguard), and **verify** generated examples against an external signal (ground truth, a reward model, execution, human review) before they enter training.
- **Synthetic pipelines work at scale when verified.** NVIDIA's Nemotron-4 340B report described generating over 98% of the alignment data with a pipeline of instruct and reward models, using only about 20,000 human-annotated examples, and released the models under a licence permitting commercial use of outputs. Read the licence of any *generator* model: some providers forbid using outputs to train competing models.

**Enterprise pattern.** Treat synthetic data as a governed dataset: record the generator model, version, prompt and seed examples (provenance), apply the same PII and licence checks as real data, filter with executable checks where possible (run the code, validate the schema, solve the problem), measure diversity (duplicate rate, embedding spread) and contamination against your evaluation sets, and hold out *real* data for evaluation so synthetic-trained models are never judged on synthetic tests alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Generating at scale without a taxonomy**, producing near-duplicates.
- **No verification**, training on confident mistakes.
- **Evaluating on synthetic data** from the same generator.
- **Training only on synthetic data**, courting collapse.
- **Skipping the real-data baseline**, so the benefit is never proven.
